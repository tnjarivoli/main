Quality + Growth + Reasonable Valuation

A step-by-step guide to the long-term investing formula. By the end you should know what to check, how to calculate it, what number is good, and how to combine all three into one decision.

Why all three are needed

Over a long period, your return from a stock comes from three sources:

Your return = Earnings growth + Change in P/E multiple + Dividend yield

  • Earnings growth is the main engine over 5 to 10 years. This is the Growth part.
  • Quality decides whether that growth is real, repeatable and survives bad years. Without quality, growth is temporary or even false.
  • Valuation decides the P/E change. If you pay too much, the P/E falls over time and eats your earnings growth. If you pay a fair price, earnings growth flows to you.

Part 1: Quality

Quality has three layers. Check all three. A company can have a great product but a dishonest management, or honest management but a weak business.

1A. Business quality

  1. Understand how it makes money. Can you explain in 2 lines who pays, for what, and why they come back? If not, do not invest.
  2. Moat (lasting advantage). Identify which one it has: brand, low cost, technology or patents, switching cost (hard for customers to leave), network effect, regulatory licence, distribution reach. The test: can a new competitor with money copy it in 3 years?
  3. Pricing power. Did it raise prices in the last few years without losing volume? Margins that hold when raw material costs rise are the proof.
  4. Demand type. Repeat or essential demand (consumables, utilities, insurance) is safer than one-time or fashion demand.
  5. Customer and supplier concentration. No single customer or supplier should be more than about 20-25% of revenue or cost.
  6. Industry structure. Is the industry cyclical, regulated, or facing disruption? Sun TV's problem in the video is structural: audiences moving to OTT and YouTube.
  7. Capital intensity. Businesses that grow without needing huge new capital (asset-light) are usually higher quality than those that must keep spending to stand still.

Understand ROE properly (DuPont).

ROE = Net margin x Asset turnover x Financial leverage

A high ROE can come from good margins, efficient use of assets, or just heavy debt. Only the first two are quality. If ROE is high mainly because of leverage, treat it with caution.

Consistency matters more than one good year. Look at the 10-year ROE, ROCE and margin lines. Quality companies show a steady line. Cyclical or weak companies zig-zag.

Accounting red flags: frequent auditor changes, qualified audit opinions, large related-party transactions, large contingent liabilities, capital work-in-progress that never gets capitalized, sudden changes in accounting policy, big gaps between profit and cash flow.

Sector note: for banks and NBFCs use ROA (around 1.5%+ for banks is strong), ROE, net interest margin, gross and net NPA, capital adequacy and P/B instead of ROCE and debt to equity. Debt is their raw material, so D/E does not apply the same way.

1C. Management quality

  • Promoter holding and trend: stable or rising is good. Steady selling needs an explanation.
  • Pledged shares: low or zero. High pledge means the promoter has borrowed against shares and a price fall can force selling.
  • Capital allocation: do they invest in the core business at good returns, avoid unrelated diversification, and repay debt in good times?
  • Track record of promises: compare guidance in past earnings calls with what happened. Repeated misses mean low trust.
  • Honesty in communication: direct answers on bad news. Evasive answers are a signal.
  • Related-party dealings and pay: excessive salaries or deals with promoter companies take value away from you.
  • Shareholder treatment: reasonable dividends, no dilutive issues at low prices, buybacks only when sensible.
  • Succession and depth: a business that depends on one person is riskier.

Quality verdict

Write one line each for business, financials and management. A stock passes Quality when: it has an understandable business with a moat, ROE and ROCE of about 15%+ that is consistent, cash flow that backs profit, manageable debt, and management with a clean record. If any single layer fails badly (for example dishonest management), stop. No growth or low price can fix that.

Part 2: Growth

Growth means growth in earnings per share (EPS) over many years. Revenue growth alone is not enough.

2A. Measure past growth correctly

CAGR = (Ending value / Starting value)^(1 / years) - 1

  • Calculate CAGR for revenue, operating profit and EPS over 3, 5 and 10 years.
  • Good sign: EPS CAGR is equal to or higher than revenue CAGR (margins stable or rising), and the 3-year number is not far weaker than the 10-year number.
  • Check the latest quarters too (YoY) to see whether growth is continuing or slowing.
  • Avoid starting-point tricks. If the first year was a bad year, the CAGR looks great. Look at the whole line.

2B. Judge the quality of growth

  1. Source: is it from volume (selling more), price (raising prices), new products, new markets or acquisitions? Volume plus new markets is the strongest. Price-only growth fades. Acquisition-only growth needs checking of the price paid.
  2. Operating leverage: when revenue grows faster than costs, profit grows faster than revenue. This is positive if sustainable.
  3. Return on new capital: when the company spends on expansion, do returns (ROCE) stay high? Growth that needs ever more capital at falling returns destroys value.
  4. Funding: growth funded by internal cash is healthier than growth funded by debt or repeated share issues.
  5. Working capital: if growth needs receivables and inventory to rise much faster than sales, cash will be stuck.
  6. One-offs: remove exceptional gains, tax write-backs and other income from profit growth.
  7. Cyclical or structural: a cyclical upswing is not long-term growth. Ask what the growth looks like through a full cycle.

2C. Estimate future growth (visibility)

  • Runway: how big is the market and what share does the company hold? A company with 2% share of a growing market has room. One with 60% share in a saturated market does not.
  • Visible drivers: order book, capacity under construction and when it starts, new product launches, regulatory changes, industry demand trends.
  • Sustainable growth rate:

Sustainable growth = ROE x Retention ratio (share of profit kept in the business)

Example: ROE 20% and 70% profit retained gives about 14% growth from internal funds. Growth far above this needs debt, share issues or higher returns.

  • Management guidance: use it as an input, then discount it by their past accuracy.
  • Make three cases: bear (low growth), base and bull. Use the base case to value, and check what the bear case does to your return.
  • Time horizon: the video suggests assessing the next 3 to 5 years. Beyond 5 years estimates become very uncertain, so do not rely on them.
Growth verdict: you can write 2 lines on why EPS will be meaningfully higher in 3 to 5 years, what drives it, what it needs (capital, approvals), and what could stop it. If you cannot, the growth part fails.

Part 3: Reasonable valuation

"Reasonable" does not mean cheapest. It means the price does not already include all the good news, so that your return can come from earnings growth and not depend on the P/E rising further.

3B. Compare in three ways

  1. Against its own history: today's P/E versus its 5- and 10-year median. More than about 20-25% above the median needs better growth to justify it.
  2. Against peers: choose 3 to 4 genuinely similar companies. Compare P/E together with ROE, growth and debt. In the video RR Kabel at P/E 50 with ROE 20.8% is not directly comparable to Finolex at P/E 26.8 with ROE 12.3%, because quality differs.
  3. Against value itself: estimate what the stock could return (see 3C). This is the most important comparison.

3C. Estimate your expected return (simple method)

  1. Take today's EPS (clean, no one-offs). For cyclical firms use average or mid-cycle EPS.
  2. Project EPS for 5 years using your base-case growth rate.
  3. Choose a conservative exit P/E for year 5 (usually its long-term median, or lower if growth will have slowed).
  4. Future price = Year-5 EPS x Exit P/E.
  5. Expected annual return:

Return = (Future price / Today's price)^(1/5) - 1 + Dividend yield

Compare with your required return (for example 12-15% a year) and with a safe option such as a fixed deposit or government bond. If the base case does not clear your required return, and the bear case loses money, the price is not reasonable yet.

3D. Reverse thinking

Ask: "What growth is already priced in?" Take today's P/E and find how many years of what growth is needed for the P/E to fall to a normal level (say 25). If the answer is "30% growth for 7 years", the price assumes perfection.

3E. Valuation traps

  • Peak-earnings trap (cyclicals): low P/E at the top of the cycle. GE Shipping at P/E 5.83 is the video's example, because freight rates are high due to conflict. Use normalized earnings and be careful with low P/E on cyclicals. Often the best time to buy a cyclical is when P/E looks high because earnings are depressed, and only if the balance sheet is strong.
  • Value trap: low P/E on a business with shrinking future. Sun TV at 15.8 is the video's example.
  • Story premium: very high P/E justified only by a story, such as Cupid at about 350.
  • Cheap versus peers because it deserves to be: lower ROE and slower growth explain a lower P/E.
  • One-off profit: P/E looks low because of an asset sale or exceptional gain.
  • Ignoring debt: a low P/E with huge debt is not cheap. Check EV/EBITDA.

Margin of safety: aim to buy at a price where even a modest miss in your estimates still gives an acceptable return. Staggered buying (3-4 tranches) gives extra protection.

Part 4: Combining the three, step by step

  1. Pick the stock and write its business in 2 lines. If you cannot, stop.
  2. Quality, part 1: check ROE, ROCE, margin consistency over 10 years.
  3. Quality, part 2: check debt, interest cover, cash conversion, receivables, other income, dilution.
  4. Quality, part 3: check moat, promoter holding, pledge, track record, red flags. Apply the veto rules below.
  5. Growth, part 1: calculate 3-, 5- and 10-year CAGR of revenue and EPS. Check the last 6 quarters.
  6. Growth, part 2: judge the quality of growth, runway, funding and drivers. Write bear, base and bull growth rates.
  7. Valuation, part 1: note P/E, EV/EBITDA, P/B as suited. Compare with own history and peers.
  8. Valuation, part 2: run the 5-year expected return calculation. Compare with your required return.
  9. Check the price context: has it risen 40% or more recently? Where is support? Plan the accumulation zone and tranches.
  10. Score and decide using the sheet below, and write the "what would make me sell" line before you buy.

Scoring sheet (100 points)

Veto rules (any one means do not buy, whatever the score):
  • Management integrity doubts or serious accounting red flags.
  • ROE below 10% for years (for a non-financial company) with no clear fix.
  • Cash flow persistently far below profit.
  • High promoter pledge or debt that threatens survival.
  • Business facing structural decline.
  • Price already up 40% or more in a short period with no pullback (wait, as per the FOMO rule).

Worked examples with numbers

Example 1: the expected return calculation (hypothetical company)

Company A: price 800, EPS 40, so P/E is 20. You expect 15% EPS growth for 5 years, dividend yield 1%.

  • Year-5 EPS = 40 x 1.15^5 = about 80.5
  • If exit P/E is 18: price = 80.5 x 18 = about 1,449. Return = (1,449/800)^(1/5) - 1 = about 12.6% a year, plus 1% dividend = about 13.6%.
  • If exit P/E falls to 14: price = about 1,127. Return is only about 7.1% a year plus dividend.

Lesson: the same 15% earnings growth gives very different returns depending on the P/E you exit at. That is why valuation matters even for a good company.

Example 2: why a very high P/E is dangerous

Using the video's Cupid P/E of about 350 as an illustration: suppose earnings grow 30% a year for 5 years. EPS becomes 3.7 times today's. With the price unchanged, P/E would still be about 94. For the P/E to reach a still-high 40, either the price must stay flat for many more years or earnings must keep growing at that pace. This is the "Greater Fool" risk the video describes.

Monitoring, selling and common mistakes

After buying: monitor each quarter

  • Is revenue, profit and margin growth on the expected path?
  • Are ROE, ROCE and cash conversion holding?
  • Any change in debt, pledge, promoter holding, auditors or management?
  • Is the moat getting stronger or weaker? Any new disruption?
  • Has valuation moved far above your exit P/E assumption?

When to review or sell

  • The reason you bought (growth driver, moat) is broken.
  • Quality deteriorates: falling ROE, rising debt, accounting or governance problems.
  • Valuation becomes so high that expected future return is poor, even if the company is fine. Consider trimming rather than a full exit.
  • A clearly better opportunity exists (compare expected returns).

Do not sell only because of a market-wide fall (FII selling, crude oil, geopolitics) if the business is intact. Do review if the business changes.

Common mistakes

  • Buying because the price is rising, not because earnings are rising.
  • Treating low P/E as proof of cheapness.
  • Looking at one year or one quarter instead of 5-10 years.
  • Comparing P/E across different businesses.
  • Ignoring cash flow, debt and other income.
  • Believing management guidance without checking past accuracy.
  • Paying any price for a great company.
  • Putting the whole amount in at one price, and over-sizing one stock.
  • Not writing down the reason to buy and the reason to sell.

Expert self-test

You understand the formula well when you can answer these without notes:

  1. What are the three sources of a stock's return, and which one does valuation control?
  2. Why can high ROE come from leverage, and how do you check?
  3. Why compare operating cash flow with net profit?
  4. How do you calculate a 5-year EPS CAGR, and why avoid a bad starting year?
  5. What is sustainable growth, and what does growth far above it imply?
  6. Why is a P/E of 6 sometimes riskier than a P/E of 50?
  7. How do you estimate expected return from EPS growth and exit P/E?
  8. What does a PEG above 2 tell you?
  9. Which metrics replace ROCE and D/E for a bank?
  10. Which veto rule would stop you even with a high total score?

Practice by running this process on 5 companies you already follow, writing the scores, and checking again after 2 to 3 quarters whether your reasoning held. That feedback is what turns the knowledge into skill.
